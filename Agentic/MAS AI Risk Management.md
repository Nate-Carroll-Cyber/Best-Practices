# AI Risk Management Process

Derived from the Monetary Authority of Singapore's *Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management* (7 October 2026)

## Scope and status

- **The Guidelines are supervisory expectations, not law.** MAS issues them under its guidelines power and reads them alongside existing legislation, the FEAT principles, and the Technology Risk Management Guidelines (¶1.2, fn 15, fn 47). They apply to all financial institutions as defined in Section 2 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2022, and on a group basis for locally incorporated FIs under consolidated supervision or that own critical information infrastructure (¶1.1, ¶1.2, fn 1, fn 4, fn 5).
- **Two effective dates.** The Guidelines take effect on 7 October 2027. Sections 3 and 4 (oversight, identification, inventory, materiality assessment) apply from that date. Sections 5 and 6 (life cycle controls, capability and infrastructure) apply by 7 October 2028 (¶1.8).
- **The six-step order is a synthesis.** MAS structures the document as oversight (§3), key systems and policies (§4), life cycle controls (§5), and capability and capacity (§6). This guide arranges those expectations in the order an FI would act on them, with the proportionality gate from §2 placed first.
- **Proportionality has two tiers.** An FI whose AI use is unlikely to have a material adverse impact may apply the basic governance set in ¶2.5 instead of the full Guidelines (¶2.3). Everything else applies in full, calibrated to risk materiality (¶2.2).
- **Every action cites its paragraph.** Blocks marked *working practice* are this guide's suggestion and are not in the Guidelines.
- **Definitions used throughout.** AI is a machine-based system or model that derives outputs through learned premises; rule-based tools and fixed-logic RPA are out of scope (¶1.3, fn 6). A model converts assumptions and data into outputs, a system comprises one or more models plus other components, and a use case is the real-world context a model or system is applied to (¶1.4). Third-party AI is any AI, or service incorporating AI, that an external party develops, owns, operates or provides and the FI relies on (fn 10). An AI incident is a failure, malfunction or poor performance of the underlying system or model (fn 20). A kill switch is a mechanism to deactivate AI expeditiously when it exceeds risk tolerances (fn 29).

## Step 1. Decide the tier and set oversight

**Decide whether the basic tier applies.** The basic tier is available only if poor performance or unavailability of the AI the FI uses is unlikely to have a material adverse impact on the FI, its customers or other stakeholders, judged on financial, operational, regulatory, legal and reputational impact to the FI and on fairness, ethical conduct and consumer protection impact to customers (¶2.3). MAS gives six examples that would generally qualify, all of them assistive tasks where a human reviews the output before use. These are drafting or rephrasing customer emails, summarising documents or meeting notes, initial document review, generating formulas or charts, image generation for marketing or internal materials, and chatbots that locate internal policies (¶2.4, fn 13).

**If the basic tier applies, put six things in place.** A named member of senior management accountable for AI oversight, a statement of permitted and prohibited uses including a ban on entering confidential, proprietary or client information into public AI tools and the circumstances that require human review before use, an approved tool list with a process for approving new tools, staff education on the policy, regular compliance checks, and a periodic and trigger-based review of whether the ¶2.3 criteria still hold (¶2.5). Nothing else in this guide is required for an FI on the basic tier until that review fails.

**Otherwise, have the board own the approach.** The board or its delegated committee approves and regularly reviews the governance approach, strategic direction and key frameworks for AI risk management, ensures AI risk is explicitly addressed in the risk appetite framework with qualitative statements and quantitative measures as appropriate, sets roles and responsibilities for board and senior management oversight, ensures it understands AI well enough to challenge, and reviews all of this against new AI developments and changes in the FI's risk profile and strategy (¶3.4). MAS offers three example quantitative measures, which are thresholds on the number or financial impact of AI incidents, the number of material use cases dependent on a single provider, and the number of material use cases breaching performance thresholds (fn 18).

**Have senior management run it.** Senior management implements the frameworks consistent with risk appetite, reviews their effectiveness against AI developments and regulatory change, institutes strategies and controls across the whole life cycle, sets roles across business lines and control functions including the three designated control functions in Step 2 and Step 3, establishes an escalation process for material risks and exceptions such as AI incidents and threshold breaches, updates the board on material issues in a timely manner, and ensures competent personnel and adequate human, technological and financial resources with training (¶3.5). The capability expectations in §6 sit here as well. Determine and ensure the competence and conduct of everyone who develops, deploys or uses AI, recruit and train for it, and review those programmes regularly so they cover newer AI technologies (¶6.1, ¶6.2).

**Keep the existing risk frameworks in play.** The board and senior management must ensure that existing frameworks for model, operational, reputational, data, technology and cybersecurity, third-party, legal and compliance, financial, conduct and environmental risk adequately identify, assess and address AI risk, and must coordinate across functions whether AI risk is managed centrally or through existing functions (¶3.2, ¶3.3, fn 14, fn 16). Using AI never relaxes other MAS requirements such as the Guidelines on Fair Dealing (fn 15).

**If relying on a group framework, keep local accountability.** A group framework may be used if it meets the Guidelines (¶1.5). Local senior management remains accountable for local considerations and Singapore-specific requirements, must have visibility and input on matters affecting Singapore operations with access to timely reports and data, must have escalation paths for Singapore-specific issues, and must be able to demonstrate to MAS how it discharges oversight (¶3.6). The delegated committee for a group FI may be a regional or global committee that oversees the Singapore FI (fn 17).

## Step 2. Identify AI and build the inventory

**Identify AI consistently across the whole FI.** Establish systems, policies and procedures with clear definitions, criteria and processes, supported by tools where appropriate, to identify AI use in every business and functional area (¶4.2). Identification is the prerequisite for everything that follows. Cover AI embedded in or used to deliver third-party services, at minimum for material third-party service providers, including software sold without an AI label that carries AI features (¶4.2, fn 21).

**Designate the control function.** The designated control function owns the identification systems and process, retains independent oversight so the process is applied consistently and catches new AI use over time, is the final arbiter on whether a given use is AI, keeps documentation of the process and outcomes, and reviews the process regularly for newer AI technologies and for AI newly introduced into existing third-party services (¶4.3).

**Treat unidentified AI as a residual risk.** Where AI cannot be consistently identified, for example because third-party disclosure is inadequate or staff use shadow AI, identify the risks that result and put mitigants in place so that the residual risk stays within appetite (¶4.4). MAS names two kinds of mitigant, which are clear staff guidance on allowed and disallowed uses including embedded features and public tools, and technical controls such as network monitoring and data loss prevention to detect unauthorised use (fn 22).

**Build and maintain the inventory.** Maintain an accurate inventory of AI use cases, systems or models across the FI to the extent practicable, with policies on maintenance and an update frequency that captures new, changed and decommissioned entries (¶4.5). The inventory may extend an existing inventory or stand alone, but in either case it must link to related inventories such as data inventories and third-party or outsourcing registers through consistent identifiers (¶4.6, fn 23).

**Capture the attributes.** The Guidelines list the attributes an inventory may hold and leave the FI to fix the set. Any differences in attributes across entries must be delineated in policy, and omissions must not weaken oversight (¶4.7, fn 24). Review the design regularly for attributes that newer technologies need (¶4.8).

| Attribute group | Attributes named in ¶4.7 and fn 25 |
|---|---|
| Identity and purpose | Purpose and description, approved scope of use such as jurisdiction, model type |
| Inputs and dependencies | Data used, dependencies |
| Status and risk | Life cycle status, assigned risk materiality rating, model review status |
| Accountability | Key roles and responsibilities such as owners and developers, links to essential documentation |
| Agent-specific | Agent identifiers, the tools and systems the agent can access, its components, the guardrails imposed |

**Designate the inventory control function.** A control function sets the inventory policies and procedures and retains independent oversight of accuracy and completeness, while business units may do the maintenance (¶4.9).

## Step 3. Assess risk materiality

**Apply one methodology to every use case.** Establish an assessment methodology based on the nature of the FI's business and the risks of the systems and models used, and apply it consistently to each AI use case (¶4.10). Dependencies between systems or models that support or interact with the use case count toward its risk profile (fn 26).

**Assess inherent and residual materiality.** Assess inherent materiality before controls and residual materiality after controls, and confirm that residual materiality meets the FI's risk appetite before deployment. Review both the methodology and each assessment regularly (¶4.11).

**Score the three dimensions** (¶4.12).

| Dimension | What to assess |
|---|---|
| Impact | Consequences of failure, malfunction or poor performance for the FI (financial, operational, regulatory, reputational) and for customers and other stakeholders (fairness, ethical breaches, consumer protection), plus the nature and sensitivity of the data processed |
| Complexity | The nature of the AI technology, the novelty of its application, the data it uses, the explainability of its outputs, and for third-party AI the level of visibility into the technology and data. This dimension can move over time as understanding of a technology matures |
| Reliance | The level of reliance on the AI including third-party AI for the decision or output, the extent of autonomy granted, and the degree of human involvement or oversight |

**Designate the materiality control function.** A control function establishes the framework, arbitrates or approves the materiality rating of each use case, keeps assessments documented, and reviews them for accuracy and relevance (¶4.13).

**Know what the Guidelines do not define.** There is no prescribed scale and no definition of "high risk materiality." The FI sets its own levels. The Guidelines then key the following to the rating.

| Consequence | Keyed to | Paragraph |
|---|---|---|
| Contingency plans with fallback options, tested regularly, and tested kill switch activation protocols where kill switches exist | High materiality | ¶5.3 |
| Degree of transparency and explainability | Materiality and context, with credit decisioning, underwriting, advisory and fund management named as high | ¶5.6, fn 12 |
| Fairness controls and fairness assessments | Materiality and context, with credit and underwriting named | ¶5.7, ¶5.8 |
| Degree of human oversight | Materiality and the objective of the AI use | ¶5.9 |
| Third-party onboarding, development and deployment controls | Materiality of the use case | ¶5.10 |
| Depth of selection documentation | Materiality, with more for complex or less understood choices | ¶5.12 |
| Depth of evaluation and testing | Materiality | ¶5.14 |
| Rigour of reproducibility documentation | Materiality | ¶5.17 |
| Scope and independence of pre-deployment review | Materiality, with formal independent validation required for high | ¶5.18, ¶5.19 |
| Peer review instead of independent validation | Not high | ¶5.20 |
| Frequency and intensity of post-deployment monitoring | Materiality | ¶5.23 |
| Kill switches or override mechanisms | High materiality, as a consideration | ¶5.23(b) |
| Regular independent re-validation | High materiality | ¶5.24 |
| Significance threshold for changes, and compensating controls where significant changes occur without prior review | Materiality | ¶5.25(a) |
| Relevance of individual life cycle controls | Materiality and relevance to the use case, with data, safety and cybersecurity controls always relevant | ¶2.2(b), fn 12 |

## Step 4. Build the controls

**Plan controls across the whole life cycle.** Assign roles for every control across business and functional units, review the controls as newer technologies arrive, and where a third-party provider's disclosure leaves gaps, identify the resulting risk and mitigate it, including by limiting the use of the AI, so that residual risk stays within appetite (¶5.1, ¶5.2). Partial deployments such as pilots and phased rollouts may deviate from the standard controls only under written policy that sets time and user limits, success criteria, terms of use, close monitoring of usage and outputs, and scope compliance (fn 28).

**Data management.** Apply general data governance for ownership, access and intellectual property to AI data and uplift it where AI needs more (¶5.4). Assess seven areas, which are fitness for purpose including against fairness, representativeness across real-world and stressed conditions, quality covering relevance, accuracy, completeness and recency with monitoring for anomalies, drift and bias, classification by criticality and sensitivity, security covering encryption in transit and at rest and timely destruction of training data, model artefacts and outputs on decommissioning, privacy including consent for sensitive personal data under PDPC guidance, and auditability and lineage covering sourcing, selection, processing, fitness and representativeness assessments, approvals and remedial actions (¶5.4(a) to (g), fn 30).

**Transparency and explainability.** Set controls proportionate to the use case and its materiality (¶5.5, ¶5.6). For use cases needing more, attend to the data features used and the justification for each, the user's ability to identify key drivers of an output, informing customers that AI is used, the consequences of AI-driven decisions, and channels for redress, and pitch disclosures to the audience so that customers get how the AI affects them and the basis for decisions while internal reviewers get model behaviour and limitations (¶5.6, fn 34). MAS points to the NIST AI RMF and IMDA's Transparency Guidelines for Generative AI Chatbots as references (fn 31, fn 34).

**Fairness.** Define what the FI considers fair outcomes, taking reference from FEAT, and put controls in place to identify and mitigate harmful bias across the life cycle (¶5.7, fn 35). Where relevant, run fairness assessments that define protected attributes and their proxies, test for systematic disadvantage with appropriate metrics, and document results and mitigations (¶5.8, fn 36).

**Human oversight.** Put controls in place proportionate to materiality and the objective of the AI, accounting for automation bias and decision fatigue as speed and scale increase (¶5.9). Assign roles with escalation and decision processes, give the people monitoring AI the authority and ability to intervene, design the system from the outset to escalate outputs for human review when pre-defined reliability or accuracy conditions are met, and keep logs from development through incident management while reviewing oversight decisions, interventions, incidents and near misses for effectiveness (¶5.9(a) to (d)). The expectation applies to every case where the FI has judged human involvement necessary, whether in the loop or over the loop (fn 37).

**Third-party AI.** Onboarding, development and deployment controls must match the materiality of the use case, contracts must give risk-appropriate visibility into the introduction of AI and later changes, third-party AI must be tested in the FI's own use cases with the FI's own data, compensatory testing must fill disclosure gaps, and the FI must document how it tested and approved the AI (¶5.10, fn 38). The FI retains primary accountability and must limit, suspend or replace the provider if residual materiality cannot be brought within appetite (¶5.11). MAS's outsourcing and third-party expectations apply in parallel (fn 39). Assess eight areas before onboarding (¶5.11(a) to (h)).

| Area | What to assess |
|---|---|
| Transparency | Whether provider disclosures let the FI judge the provider's life cycle controls, and whether independent certifications or external assessments, not self-attestations, or compensating testing and heavier human oversight can fill the gaps |
| Supply chain | Whether third-party and open-source models, datasets and dependencies have had supply chain assessments covering provenance, training data integrity and known vulnerabilities |
| Concentration | Whether reliance on key providers, direct or indirect, creates concentration risk |
| Change management | Whether the FI receives notice of post-deployment updates and can assess their impact |
| Contingency | Whether plans cover failure, unexpected behaviour and vendor discontinuation, especially for high materiality use |
| Legal agreements | Whether contracts set performance guarantees, data protection, audit rights, notification of AI introduction and updates, and consent before AI is incorporated |
| Capabilities | Whether procurement, development, deployment and user staff understand third-party AI |
| Complexity | Whether the FI has experience with this class of service, with deeper evaluation and cybersecurity checks where it does not |

**Selection.** Consider the objectives and risks of the use case when choosing systems, models or features, document the reasoning in proportion to materiality and especially when choosing complex or less understood options over simpler ones, balance performance against complexity, fairness and explainability, draw on research and industry practice, and include domain expert or user review (¶5.12, fn 41, fn 42). Where newer and less understood AI is chosen, weigh the benefit against hallucination, opaqueness, security risk and the FI's capacity to mitigate them (¶5.13).

## Step 5. Test, secure and review before deployment

**Evaluate and test in proportion to materiality.** Identify the key risks of the use case, set clear measurable thresholds, and assess reliability and safety in context against best practice across real-world and edge conditions (¶5.14). Define evaluation measures aligned to the AI's objectives with performance thresholds agreed by business owners, developers and reviewers (¶5.14(a)). Use out-of-sample and out-of-time testing, sensitivity and stability analysis, sub-population analysis, stress testing including edge cases and adversarial testing where appropriate, error analysis and benchmarking against alternatives, with test data representative of the FI's context (¶5.14(b)). Mitigate overfitting by preferring simpler models unless complexity earns a clear uplift, regularisation, feature selection and cross-validation (¶5.14(c), fn 44).

**Put guardrails on known limitations before deployment.** Where development surfaces risks and limitations, apply controls and guardrails before go-live, which may include a guarded pilot or phased rollout (¶5.15, fn 45). For generative AI and agents, testing must cover key failure modes and guardrail effectiveness, with attention to hallucination, toxic or harmful or biased content, data leakage and adversarial vulnerability (¶5.15, fn 46). MAS points to IMDA's Starter Kit for Testing LLM-Based Applications as a voluntary reference (fn 43, fn 46).

**Secure the system.** Deploy into secured environments with hardened configurations, network segmentation, input validation, API authentication, encryption and data loss prevention (¶5.16(a)). Control access to AI components and infrastructure with role-based access, multi-factor authentication and privileged access management, and consider separating the teams that train and test (¶5.16(b)). Govern third-party plugins, APIs and services, restrict their data exposure, and monitor them for security, compliance and operational issues (¶5.16(c)). The Technology Risk Management Guidelines apply in full (fn 47). Ensure the GPU, network, memory and data pipeline infrastructure meets performance, scalability and resilience needs (¶6.3).

**Document for reproducibility.** Document the development process in enough detail that an independent reviewer or auditor could understand and potentially replicate the implementation and its testing, covering data sources and processing and quality checks, selection rationale, training procedures with code versions, environments and hyperparameters, evaluation measures and thresholds and results, explainability and fairness analyses, and key assumptions, limitations and mitigants (¶5.17).

**Review before deployment with parties outside development.** Every use case gets a pre-deployment review by people not involved in its development, under written policy, assessing suitability, robustness and performance within defined boundaries (¶5.18). High materiality use cases get formal independent validation by competent personnel independent of development and deployment, providing effective challenge across conceptual soundness, data suitability and quality, implementation integrity, evaluation and testing, explainability and fairness, and assumptions and limitations (¶5.19). Other use cases may use documented peer review by qualified individuals not involved in development or deployment (¶5.20, fn 48). Reviewers report findings, limitations, remediation and conditions of use to the approval body, which ensures recommendations are actioned (¶5.21).

**Run the technology and cybersecurity review.** Review the architecture including APIs, plugins and third-party services for secure design, environment segregation and least-privilege access, and complete a go-live checklist confirming that encryption, data loss prevention, firewalls, access restrictions and logging are operational (¶5.22(a)). Run vulnerability assessments, penetration testing and red teaming, test against manipulation, evasion and poisoning, and implement input validation, throttling and anomaly detection (¶5.22(b)).

## Step 6. Monitor, manage change and retire

**Monitor all deployed AI, including third-party AI.** Define metrics and thresholds per use case covering robustness, stability, data quality and fairness, use tiered early-warning thresholds, check for data drift, concept drift and model drift, and where relevant monitor the information flow and decision paths of AI workflows including reasoning processes, actions taken and tools used (¶5.23(a)).

**Manage incidents.** Establish reporting, tracking, escalation and resolution for breaches and anomalies, with resolution ranging from retraining through redevelopment to decommissioning (¶5.23(b)). For high materiality AI, consider kill switches or override mechanisms. Give users of deployed AI a way to report issues and give feedback (¶5.23(b)). Assign an accountable person for monitoring and incident management (¶5.23(c)). Keep records of monitoring, issues and remediation, strictly control and log access to systems, training data, pipelines and configuration, and for generative AI and agents consider logging prompts, responses, model versions and reasoning processes (¶5.23(d)). Train the monitors and train users to recognise and report unintended behaviour (¶5.23(e)). Senior management's escalation process from Step 1 receives material incidents and threshold breaches (¶3.5(e)).

**Keep contingency plans live for high materiality use.** Maintain fallback options such as alternative systems or manual processes, test them across a range of operating conditions, and where kill switches exist keep activation protocols defined and tested (¶5.3).

**Re-validate on a schedule and on triggers.** High materiality use cases get regular independent re-validation. Any use case gets re-validated or reviewed when interconnected systems show issues, monitoring breaches thresholds, the AI or its environment changes significantly, or new external risks appear from regulatory or technological developments (¶5.24).

**Control change.** Set a framework that grades the significance of a change by its nature and the use case's materiality, routes significant changes through review and re-approval before implementation, and applies compensating controls such as enhanced monitoring where significant changes may land without prior review (¶5.25(a), fn 49). Prevent unauthorised alteration with change control under human oversight and version control that covers code, data, parameters, hyperparameters and other artefacts with traceability and rollback (¶5.25(b)). For self-updating AI, require strict justification for enabling automatic updates, define what may change automatically such as retraining or hyperparameters but not core architecture, and add data quality checks and tighter performance monitoring (¶5.25(c)).

**Retire deliberately.** Retire or decommission AI when it is no longer needed or exceeds risk tolerance, with controls that address dependencies, data retention, secure removal from production and stakeholder notification (¶5.26). Destroy or sanitise training data, model artefacts and outputs once no longer required (¶5.4(e)). Update the inventory to reflect decommissioning (¶4.5).

## What the Guidelines leave open

- **No materiality scale.** The three dimensions are named and the consequences of a high rating are listed, but the number of levels, their definitions and the boundary of "high" are left to each FI's methodology.
- **No numeric thresholds.** Risk appetite measures, performance thresholds, monitoring thresholds and change significance are all left to the FI. The three quantitative risk appetite measures in fn 18 are examples.
- **No log retention period and no MAS incident reporting trigger.** Logging and records are required throughout, but no retention floor is set and incident escalation is internal. Reporting to MAS falls under other notices and is not addressed here.
- **No prescribed test suite.** Evaluation methods are listed by name and the IMDA Starter Kit is offered as a voluntary reference. Adversarial testing and red teaming are expected but not specified.
- **Thin agent-specific content.** Agents appear in the risk discussion (¶1.11), the inventory attributes (fn 25), the monitoring of reasoning and tool use (¶5.23(a)) and the logging of prompts and reasoning (¶5.23(d)). Tool authorisation, agent identity, inter-agent interaction and autonomy limits are not addressed, and MAS points to IMDA's Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI instead (fn 11).
- **Kill switches are a consideration, not a requirement.** They are expected to be tested where they exist (¶5.3) and considered for high materiality AI (¶5.23(b)).
- **No inventory schema.** The attribute list in ¶4.7 is permissive, and the FI must document which attributes it captures and why.
- **No treatment of model-originated behaviour.** The Guidelines frame AI risk as failure, malfunction, poor performance, bias and compromise. Reward hacking, evaluation gaming, sandbagging and other strategic behaviours are not named, and the closest hook is the agent goal-translation divergence in ¶1.11.

# Appendix. MAS Guidelines on AI Risk Management mapped to CSA AICM v1.0.3

The Guidelines contain no risk catalogue, so the mapping unit is the expectation. Rows are the 13 risk examples in ¶1.9 to ¶1.11, the six basic-tier requirements in ¶2.5, and each expectation in §3 to §6 at the paragraph or sub-paragraph level where the Guidelines give one. Coverage ratings are the author's judgment against the AICM control specifications and are not a published crosswalk. Covered means AICM controls target the named mechanism. Partial means AICM reaches part of the expectation or only generically. Gap means no AICM control addresses it. The AICM export used here (AICMv1.0.3-generated_at_2025_11_10.xlsx) carries control specifications only, with no implementation guideline or auditing guideline text, so every rating rests on specification wording and no guideline-level detail is cited.
